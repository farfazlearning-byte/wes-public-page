How it teaches

Five steps. No gaps. No rushing past a child.

A lesson is not a video to watch and forget. It is a small class that checks, at every step, whether the child has truly understood — and adapts before moving on.

1

Listen

A warm voice teaches the idea aloud, slowly, with an example from everyday life. Words light up and are underlined in approximate time with the voice — paced to the recording, so the highlighted word may sometimes run a little ahead of or behind the spoken word.

2

Read it

The same idea in short, clear lines. Read together, then read alone. For little children, poems become animated songs — watch, sing, and read the words as they light up.

3

Write it

Copy, complete and explain in the child's own words. Handwriting and thinking, together.

4

Check it

One small question. Right? Stretch further. Wrong? A simpler explanation, then try again.

5

Adapt

The next lesson starts where this one ended — never where the timetable says it should.

The promise: if a child cannot explain it in their own words, the lesson is not finished. The system slows down, re-teaches more simply, and only then moves forward.

Voices first · The two house voices

Two voices. The same two voices, in every lesson, in every language.

A word only reaches a child if it is heard exactly as it was spoken — and a child should never have to re-learn how to hear their teacher. So this system has exactly two house voices: Nisa, the ladies voice, and Arshed, the gents voice. You can learn in Nisa's voice or Arshed's voice. The learner chooses one, and that one voice carries the welcome tour, every spoken lesson, every re-teaching and every encouragement — in English, Tamil, Hindi, Spanish, French and every language that follows. Both voices are soft and sweet, warm and natural, like talking with a kind person — not flat reading aloud. In production the two voices are fixed, recorded voices, with pronunciation checked in every language. In this demo your device's speech stands in for the house voice you choose, at the pitch and pace of that house voice — that stand-in is labelled wherever it is used, and the recorded lessons keep their recorded teacher's voice.

  • Young children (about 4–7) hear slower, clearer speech: a slower pace, short sentences, a small pause after each key word, lively but calm intonation, and the important word said twice. That is how children learn new sounds — research on child-directed speech calls it clearer articulation, slower rate and repetition.
  • Growing learners (about 8–12) hear a steady, natural teaching pace, with key words stressed and sentences kept short enough to follow while reading along.
  • Older learners (13+) hear a natural pace, like a good teacher talking — no sing-song, no rushing, every word still fully formed.
  • Consistency matters: the same chosen house voice reads, re-teaches and gives the tour, lesson after lesson, language after language — children should never have to re-learn how to hear their teacher.
  • Tone carries meaning: encouragement sounds warm, a question sounds curious, a new word sounds clear and proud. The voice never shouts, never hurries a child, and never makes a mistake feel shameful. It talks with the child, the way a person talks in everyday conversation — pauses, warmth and all.

Money & Wealth · A whole-life track, for everyone

Money, taught from the very beginning — little by little, for life.

How to earn money, how to save it, how to grow it slowly, and how to use it in good ways: one track, from a child's first coin jar to an adult's investments and debts, taught in the same four steps as every lesson — listen, read, write, check. It sits beside the school lessons (Track A) and the system's own lessons (Track B), and it runs in every wave, in every country, because money habits are formed young and carried for life.

Why this order? Child-development research finds that money habits largely take shape by about age 7, starting at 3–4 with recognising money and telling needs from wants; in middle childhood (about 6–12) children can practise earning, saving towards a goal and budgeting; in the teens they can handle interest, compound growth and the idea of investing — and adults need the full picture, including debt and diversification. This ladder follows that order: nothing is taught before a child can use it, and nothing a grown-up needs is left out. And a promise this track never makes: no lesson says money will grow by any amount, or that any investment, gold or diamond will rise in value. Values can fall. Children never handle real-money investing here — practice is with play money only, and every money decision that matters is one to talk through with a grown-up.

  1. 3–5 Ages 3–5 · LKG – UKG · First money ideas

    Money, needs and wants — and a first coin jar

    Recognising coins and notes, telling a need (food, water, a home, care) from a want (a toy, a sweet), and the joy of putting one coin in a jar and watching it fill, little by little. Taught as a story, with pictures and a song-like pace — never as a lecture, and never with numbers a small child cannot hold.

    Why now: habits form earliest here, through stories and play. Not yet: no prices, no interest, no investing words — only “keep a little, use a little, share a little.”
  2. 6–8 Ages 6–8 · Classes 1–3 · Earn, save, share

    Three jars — earn, save and give — and saving towards a goal

    Money is earned by doing helpful work, it is saved in a jar for a named goal, and a little is shared with someone who needs it. Children practise counting money, setting a goal price, and working out “how many weeks, little by little?” with the practice goal tool below. Earning ideas stay safe and age-fitting — helping at home, making things with a grown-up — and are never presented as work a child must do.

    Why now: children can count, wait for a goal, and understand fairness. Not yet: no bank accounts in a child's name alone, no interest calculations, no investing.
  3. 9–12 Ages 9–12 · Classes 4–7 · Banks, budgets and wealth in all its forms

    A bank, simple interest, a budget — and gold, silver and diamonds, honestly

    What a bank does, why keeping money there is safer than under a mattress, what interest means in a worked example, and how a simple budget puts needs first. Wealth is taught in all its forms — cash, bank savings, gold, silver, diamonds, land and things — with the honest part kept in: prices go down as well as up, selling often brings back less than was paid, and a shiny promise of quick doubling is a warning sign, not an opportunity. Scam safety is taught here, plainly: never share a password or one-time code, never send money to a stranger, and always tell a grown-up.

    Why now: children handle real amounts and hear real offers. Not yet: no investing decisions, no real accounts — examples only, with a grown-up beside them.
  4. 13–17 Ages 13–17 · Classes 8–12 · Growing money slowly, practised only

    Compound growth, investing ideas, and digital money safety

    Compound growth — earnings earning their own earnings, over years — shown with worked arithmetic, labelled as an example, never as a promise. Investing is explained in plain words (shares, bonds, funds, risk, fees, inflation) and practised only with play money: a simulated portfolio, tracked and discussed, with no real money at stake and no real-money investing for anyone under 18. Digital money safety runs through it all: UPI and card safety, fake apps and messages, and the habit of pausing and checking before any tap.

    Why now: teens make money decisions and meet digital offers daily. Not yet: no real investing, no borrowing, no product is ever recommended — principles only.
  5. 18+ Adults · Parents, teachers, everyone · Money for life

    Investing, diversification, debt dangers — and using wealth well

    The full adult picture: an emergency fund first, diversification (not putting everything in one place), the cost of debt and why high-interest borrowing grows against you, long-term thinking for later life, and wealth used well — for family, for learning, for giving, and for the community. Gold and diamonds return here as one small part of a wider picture, never the whole plan. This is general teaching, not personal financial advice: big decisions deserve a qualified adviser and a check of local rules.

    Why it never ends: money decisions keep changing — new products, new scams, new needs. The track stays with the learner for life, one new lesson at a time.

All the forms of wealth — taught honestly

“Money is the world” only if we forget what wealth really is. Children learn every form — and what can go wrong with each — so no single shiny thing is ever mistaken for safety itself.

Cash

Coins and notes, in hand. Good for: small daily spending. Honest part: cash can be lost or stolen, and as prices rise over the years the same cash buys a little less. Keeping everything in cash is not a plan.

Bank savings

Money kept safely, earning a little interest in an example. Good for: goals and emergencies. Honest part: interest is small, it changes, and it is never promised — the example in a lesson is arithmetic, not a forecast.

Gold

Jewellery, coins and bars, valued by weight and purity. Good for: a long-held store of value in many families. Honest part: the price moves up and down, making charges and selling costs mean jewellery often sells for less than was paid, and purity must always be checked. Gold is one part of wealth, never all of it.

Silver

Like gold, smaller and more changeable in price. Honest part: silver prices swing more sharply than gold, storage and selling costs matter, and “silver will only rise” is never true of any metal.

Diamonds

Beautiful, hard stones, valued by cut, colour, clarity and weight. Honest part: diamonds are the least honest “investment” of all to promise about — resale very often brings back far less than the shop price, fakes exist, and a diamond's worth is what a real buyer will pay, not what a seller hopes. Taught as beauty and keepsakes first, never as a quick way to grow money.

Land, a home and things

A home, land, tools and things that last. Honest part: they need care, tax and repair; they cannot be sold in a hurry; and their price can fall as well as rise. A home is shelter first, wealth second.

Skills, health and trust

The wealth no one can steal: what you know, what you can do, your health, and the trust of people who know you keep your word. Taught at every age: learning, kindness and honest work grow this wealth every day — and it is what makes every other kind of wealth usable.

A gentle push, inside every lesson — never pressure

While a lesson is running, the teaching voice pauses to put the idea into the learner's own life — a small question, a tiny mission, a word of encouragement. It is an invitation, never a demand: there is no score for it, no shame for skipping it, and no comparison with anyone else. The aim is a thought that stays in the mind after the lesson ends.

“You put one coin away today. That is how every big jar in the world began — one coin, then another.”Heard in the 3–5 lesson, after the first saving.

“Ask a grown-up tonight: what did our family save for, little by little? Listen to their story.”A family-talk prompt in the 6–8 lesson — the lesson grows when it is talked about at home.

“If a message promises to double your money quickly, that is the moment to stop — and to tell a grown-up. Clever people pause. That pause is the skill.”Scam safety in the 9–12 lesson, repeated until it is a habit.

Missions are tiny and real: keep a one-week coin diary, count a jar together, draw a budget for a family celebration, track a play-money portfolio for a month. Each one ends with the same words — well done for trying; trying is the whole point. Motivation without pressure, and without any get-rich-quick talk, is the rule of the whole track.

Ideas for gathering, little by little — not promises

Every idea below is taught with its limits beside it. Nothing here promises an income, and nothing asks a child to work, borrow or risk real money.

Earn

Help, make, and learn. Helpful work at home and for neighbours (with a grown-up's knowledge), things made and sold with a grown-up's help — cards, plants, repairs — and, for older learners, skills learned step by step in the livelihood tracks. Never: work that keeps a child from school, sleep or play; borrowing to start; or any offer that sounds too easy.

Save

Pay yourself first, a little, always. A fixed small amount put aside before spending, a named jar or account for each goal, and “round-up” habits — the change from a purchase goes in the jar. Little by little is not slow: it is the method. A jar that grows by a little every week is the whole lesson, drawn in real life.

Grow

Slowly, and only with practice first. For teens and adults: compound growth over years, diversification, low fees, and patience — practised with play money before any real decision, and always with the warning that values fall too. For children: growing means growing skills, which no market can take away.

Use well

Needs first, then wants; repair before replace. A budget that is written down, a pause before a want (“do I still want it tomorrow?”), mending, sharing and borrowing instead of buying new, and choosing quality that lasts over cheapness that breaks. Using money well is taught as a skill, not as giving things up.

Share

A little, given gladly. The third jar — for family, for a neighbour, for a cause the child chooses. Giving is taught as part of wealth, not as what is left over: a generous person with a little is wealthy in the way that matters most.

Practice tool · Simulated money only

My savings goal — little by little

Plan a goal with play money. No real money moves here, nothing is bought, and nothing you type is saved beyond this visit. Type any amounts you like — a storybook, a ball, a bicycle — and see how many weeks of small savings it takes. This tool only adds; it never guesses interest or returns.

A goal is an invitation, not a test. If the weeks feel too many, choose a smaller goal first — reaching a small goal teaches more than staring at a big one. Talk through any real saving with a grown-up, and never type a real bank detail, card number or password into any practice tool.

Culture & Humanity · Know your own, understand others, respect all

Every culture is learned about. Every human is equal.

Every country and every community has its own culture — its festivals, stories, food, greetings and values. This track has three parts: your own culture, learned deeply and practised freely; other cultures, learned about with respect, so people understand one another; and humanity first — the lesson, taught from childhood, that all humans are equal. When people understand each other, disagreements are easier to solve with words, and harder to turn into hatred or fighting.

How this track teaches — four rules that never change

  • About, not preaching. Lessons describe what communities do, celebrate and value, in factual words. They do not tell any learner what to believe, and they do not ask any learner to practise a faith that is not their family's.
  • No ranking. No culture or religion is placed above another, and none is described as better or worse. Every community is presented in its own terms, with the same care.
  • Freedom, with no barrier. A learner follows their own culture freely and is encouraged to practise it. Learning about other cultures is always open, and the freedom to change to any path is respected — the family chooses, and can change the choice at any time in Settings.
  • Checked with the community. Culture lessons are written to be checked by teachers and members of the community described, so that festivals, stories and customs are described as that community itself describes them.

1 · My Culture — the path your family chooses

The family picks the learner's culture in Settings — Hindu, Muslim, Christian, Buddhist, Sikh, Jain, Jewish, another culture or community, or “learning about all” if no single choice is wanted. The choice stays only in this visit, in this demo, and can be changed at any time. Whichever path is chosen, its lessons cover the same five threads: festivals, stories, food, greetings and values — taught about that community, factually and warmly, with encouragement to practise it at home and in the community, never as a test.

Your family's choice: not chosen yet — pick one in Settings, or keep learning about all cultures below.

For young children the path is festivals, food, stories, greetings and kindness — what to say, what is eaten, what is celebrated, and how to be kind. For older learners it deepens: history, a respectful overview of beliefs and practices, and how people of different cultures have lived, worked and celebrated side by side. Nothing in the path is ever a barrier to another part of the system: every lesson, tool and career path stays open to every learner.

2 · World Cultures — learning about one another, respectfully

The same five threads, for each community — described as that community describes itself. Customs vary by country, region and family; where that is so, the lessons say so. These are starting summaries for the demo; full lessons follow the same respectful format, community by community.

HinduYour family's choice

Festivals: Diwali, Holi and Navratri are celebrated by many Hindu families, in ways that vary by region. Stories: the Ramayana and Mahabharata are told in many homes. Food and greetings: food varies widely by region; many families are vegetarian; a common greeting is Namaste, with hands together. Values taught about: respect for elders, kindness, and care for living things.

MuslimYour family's choice

Festivals: Eid al-Fitr, at the end of the fasting month of Ramadan, and Eid al-Adha. Stories: stories of the prophets, including the life of the Prophet Muhammad, are told in families and mosques. Food and greetings: halal food; dates are often eaten to break the fast; a common greeting is Assalamu alaikum — “peace be upon you”. Values taught about: charity, hospitality, prayer and care for family.

ChristianYour family's choice

Festivals: Christmas and Easter, celebrated in churches and homes in many countries. Stories: the life and teachings of Jesus, as told in the Bible. Food and greetings: shared festival meals vary by country; “Peace be with you” is a greeting used in worship. Values taught about: love of neighbour, forgiveness, and helping others.

BuddhistYour family's choice

Festivals: Vesak, remembering the birth, enlightenment and passing of the Buddha. Stories: the life of Siddhartha Gautama and the Jataka tales. Food and greetings: food varies by country; many Buddhists are vegetarian; greetings vary by country, often with hands together. Values taught about: compassion, mindfulness, and not harming living things.

SikhYour family's choice

Festivals: Vaisakhi and Gurpurabs, marking the birthdays of the Gurus. Stories: the lives of Guru Nanak and the other Gurus. Food and greetings: langar — a free shared meal, open to everyone, served in the gurdwara; a common greeting is Sat Sri Akal. Values taught about: seva (selfless service), equality, honest work and sharing.

JainYour family's choice

Festivals: Mahavir Jayanti and Paryushan. Stories: the life of Mahavira and the Tirthankaras. Food and greetings: many Jains are vegetarian, with great care taken not to harm living things; a common greeting is Jai Jinendra. Values taught about: ahimsa (non-violence), truthfulness, and living simply.

JewishYour family's choice

Festivals: Passover, Hanukkah and Rosh Hashanah, celebrated in homes and synagogues. Stories: the Exodus and stories from the Torah. Food and greetings: kosher food rules are followed in many observant homes; challah bread is shared on Shabbat; a common greeting is Shalom — “peace”. Values taught about: learning, family, justice, and care for the stranger.

3 · Humanity First — taught from childhood

Above every difference is what is shared: every human needs food, water, shelter, safety, learning and kindness; every human feels joy and pain; every human deserves respect. This part is taught to every learner, at every age, alongside their own culture and the cultures of others: young children learn it through stories of kindness and sharing; older learners learn dialogue, how to listen across disagreement, and how to solve a disagreement with words and fairness — never with hatred or violence. Freedom runs through it: to follow one's own culture, to learn about others, and to change path, with respect for the choices of others too.

Move & Show · Answer with your hands

Show me with your fingers — and the lesson answers back.

Some children cannot answer in words yet — and some days, words are simply hard. So the lesson can ask a question the body can answer: “Show four fingers.” The child holds up fingers to the camera, or taps the big number buttons below. Either way the lesson counts, and answers, kindly. Movement is part of learning here, not a break from it: counting, answering yes or no, pointing, tracing and clapping all teach through the hands and the body, for children aged about 3–8.

    Pick the colours your page wears. The gold word-highlight stays the same in every theme, so follow-along words are always easy to spot.

    Text size

    Bigger words are easier on young eyes and on low vision. Hear mode also enlarges the text.

    Motion

    Calm motion stops the moving ticker and the twinkling and drifting in the song video. The words still light up as they are sung.

    Keyboard & no time pressure

    Every control on this page works with Tab, Enter and Space — including every word in a lesson. No lesson and no check is timed, in any mode. You can pause, repeat and try again as often as you like.
